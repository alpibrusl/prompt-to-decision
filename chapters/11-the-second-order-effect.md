# The Second-Order Effect

Ledgerly hires six people in six weeks to move fast on Germany. The direct effect is exactly what was modelled: more headcount, more burn, a German operation standing up on schedule. What wasn't modelled is what happens to the other twenty people already there.

## The effect the model was built to catch, and the one it wasn't

A **second-order effect** is a consequence that follows from the direct result of a decision, rather than from the decision itself — not "we hired six people," but what hiring six people fast does to onboarding capacity, to the engineering team's ability to support existing customers while also ramping new hires, to the founders' attention, which is now split between a new market and the business that's actually paying the bills today. The financial model in the recommendation almost certainly captured the direct effect precisely — that's exactly the kind of number *Prompt to Ledger* teaches you to demand and verify. It just as certainly wasn't built to capture the second-order ones because they don't show up as a line item in a hiring plan.

This isn't a failure of the model. A financial model is supposed to model the finances; asking it to also model team morale and support quality is asking it to do a job it was never built for. The failure, if there is one, is treating the model's silence on second-order effects as evidence that none exist, rather than as a gap the model was never going to fill.

## Where to look for them

Second-order effects cluster in a few predictable places, and it's worth checking each deliberately rather than hoping one occurs to you.

**Capacity that isn't headcount.** Six new hires need onboarding, mentorship, and management attention from people who are already fully occupied running the existing business. If nobody currently has spare capacity to onboard six people well, the real cost of this hire isn't just their salary — it's degraded onboarding for them and reduced attention for the work everyone else was already doing.

**Quality for the customers not part of the plan.** Existing Ledgerly customers, in the domestic market, don't care that the company is expanding into Germany. If founder and senior-engineer attention splits toward the new market, the support and product quality the existing customer base has been receiving can quietly degrade — not because anyone chose to deprioritize them, but because attention is finite and the new thing is, by definition, taking some of it.

**What the plan implicitly promises that isn't in the model.** Six people hired with the expectation of a real, resourced expansion, who discover four months in that the pilot didn't clear its threshold and the company is pulling back — that's a real cost, in trust and in the market's read of Ledgerly as an employer, that never appears in a runway calculation.

## Asking doesn't require predicting all of it

The point of this chapter isn't to demand a complete second-order model before any decision — that's neither realistic nor genuinely useful, since second-order effects are, by nature, harder to see in advance than direct ones. It's to ask the question deliberately, in the categories above, rather than letting the confidence of a well-built financial model imply that everything worth knowing is already captured in it. A decision made with "we haven't modelled what this does to onboarding capacity, and here's our honest guess" is a meaningfully better decision than one made without the question ever being asked, even though neither one produces a precise number.

## What to ask for

> "Beyond the direct financial effect, what does this decision do to the capacity, attention, or quality of things that aren't part of the plan itself?"

The core question. It won't produce a number the way the financial model does — the honest answer is often qualitative — but asking it is the difference between a blind spot and a known, accepted risk.

> "Who currently has the capacity to onboard and support six new hires well? If the answer is nobody, what does that actually cost, beyond the hires' own salaries?"

Names the capacity question specifically, since it's the most common and most underestimated second-order effect in any fast hiring plan.

> "What happens to our existing customers' experience while founder and senior attention shifts toward this new market? Is that an acceptable cost, or an unexamined one?"

Forces a decision about the trade-off between existing and new customers to be made on purpose, rather than discovered later as an accident of where attention actually went.
