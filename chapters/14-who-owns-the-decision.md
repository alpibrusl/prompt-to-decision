# Who Owns the Decision?

Every chapter in this book has been about making a recommendation trustworthy enough to act on. This one is about a different question, sitting underneath all of them: once it's trustworthy, who actually has to act?

## Delegation moves work. It cannot move ownership.

**Accountability** is being the person the consequences of a decision actually land on — not the person who proposed it, computed it, or recommended it, but the one who has to live with the outcome and answer for it. An agent can be genuinely delegated an enormous amount of the work behind a decision: building the financial model, running the analysis, comparing the alternatives, even producing a confident final recommendation. None of that delegation moves accountability anywhere. Whoever decides to actually hire the six people and sign the German lease is accountable for what happens next, regardless of how much of the thinking behind that choice the agent did honestly and well.

This is worth stating plainly because it's easy to let it drift, one small step at a time. The agent verified the code. The agent tested the analysis. The agent built and stress-tested the financial model. The agent produced a well-argued recommendation that survived real pushback. At the end of a chain like that, it can start to feel like the agent decided — like there's nobody left to hold responsible, because every individual step was handled competently by something other than a person. That feeling is exactly wrong, and it's worth naming why: verification is precisely the kind of work that can be delegated, checked, and trusted once checked. Accountability is not that kind of thing. It doesn't get established by a process being followed correctly. It exists because a specific person chose to act, knowing they would answer for the outcome.

## Ownership needs a name, not a committee

The practical failure mode here is diffusion: a decision that everyone was involved in, that the agent helped build a strong case for, that the board discussed and nobody objected to — and that, if it goes wrong, turns out to belong to nobody in particular. "We all agreed" is a comfortable thing to say before a decision and a genuinely unhelpful one after it goes wrong, because it means nobody specifically owns fixing it, explaining it, or being the person who learns from it directly.

Before committing to anything this book has helped you stress-test, name the owner explicitly — one person, or a small, specific group, who is accountable for the outcome and who has the authority to act on the threshold from Chapter 7 if it's crossed. This doesn't mean that person made every judgment alone. It means that when the German pilot's four-month mark arrives, there's a specific person whose job it is to look at the number and act, rather than a diffuse sense that someone probably should.

## What ownership actually requires

Owning a decision means three concrete things, and it's worth checking that whoever is named as the owner actually has all three, not just the title. They need the authority to act on the threshold without needing to re-litigate the whole decision when it's crossed. They need to actually be watching for it — not a founder so buried in the day-to-day of running the new market that the eight-week check quietly slips. And they need to be someone the consequences genuinely reach, not a person insulated enough from the outcome that acting decisively on bad news costs them little either way.

A well-verified recommendation, run through every earlier chapter in this book, still isn't a decision until someone with all three of those things actually makes the call.

## What to ask for

> "Who specifically owns this decision — not the company, not the board, one person or a small named group — and do they have the authority to act on our threshold when it arrives?"

The core question. A decision owned by everyone is, in practice, owned by no one when it actually matters.

> "Is the owner actually going to be watching for the threshold we set, or will it need someone else to notice and raise it? Who is that person, concretely?"

Catches the gap between naming a threshold and someone actually being positioned to act on it when it arrives.

> "If this goes wrong, who is going to be the one explaining what happened and what changes next — not in the abstract, by name?"

The most direct version of the ownership question, and often the one that reveals most clearly whether a decision has a real owner or just a plausible-sounding process behind it.
