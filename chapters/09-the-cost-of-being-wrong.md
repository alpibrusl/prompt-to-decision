# The Cost of Being Wrong

Ledgerly has twelve months of runway. The hiring and expansion plan, if it goes well, could plausibly justify itself within that window. If it goes badly, the same plan — six new hires, most of them fully ramped by month two — pushes monthly burn from roughly €40,000 to close to €79,000. Runway doesn't shrink gently in that scenario. It drops from twelve months to around six.

## The same probability, two very different bets

**Asymmetric risk** is when the upside and downside of a decision aren't mirror images of each other — when being right and being wrong don't cost and pay the same amount, even if each is equally likely. A coin flip that pays €100 on heads and costs €100 on tails is symmetric: the size of the bet doesn't change whether it's a good idea. A coin flip that pays €100 on heads and costs your ability to make payroll on tails is not the same bet, even at identical odds, because one side of it threatens something the other side's gain can't buy back.

Ledgerly's expansion has exactly this shape. If it works, the company grows meaningfully faster than it otherwise would have — a real, valuable outcome, but one of degree: faster growth instead of merely good growth. If it doesn't work, runway halves, and a company with six months of runway is in a fundamentally different, more dangerous position than one with twelve — not just a smaller version of the same situation, but one where the next fundraise has to happen under real time pressure, on worse terms, or doesn't happen at all. The upside and the downside are not the same kind of outcome wearing different sizes. One is faster success. The other is a different, more precarious game.

## A recommendation's probability is not enough on its own

An agent asked to evaluate this plan can honestly report that it's more likely to work than not — say, a genuine, well-reasoned sixty percent chance of a good outcome. That number, by itself, does not tell you whether to take the bet, because it says nothing about what the other forty percent actually costs. A sixty percent chance of a meaningfully better outcome, against a forty percent chance of a survivable inconvenience, is a good bet almost regardless of the exact odds. A sixty percent chance of a meaningfully better outcome, against a forty percent chance of running out of cash before the next raise closes, is a much harder call — and it can stay a hard call even if you nudge that sixty percent higher, because the problem was never really about the probability. It's about what sits on the other side of it.

## Sizing the downside in its own terms, not just its dollar cost

The habit worth building: before accepting a recommendation's probability of success as the thing that matters most, ask what the downside scenario actually looks like in practice, not just what it costs in euros. Runway dropping from twelve months to six is a number. What it means is a company that has to raise its next round from a position of visible weakness, or cut costs hard and fast in a way that damages morale and the product simultaneously, or, in the worst realistic case, run out of options before either of those plays out. That is a different kind of bad than "we spent some money and grew a bit slower than hoped," even though both might get summarized in a model as "the plan didn't work."

This is not an argument against taking the risk. Plenty of good decisions have real asymmetric downside and are still worth making, because the upside genuinely justifies it or because the downside, sized honestly, turns out to be more survivable than it first sounds. It's an argument against letting a single probability number stand in for a decision that actually depends on the shape of both sides of the bet, not just how likely each one is.

## What to ask for

> "If this goes wrong, what does that actually look like in practice — not the dollar cost alone, but the situation we'd be in? Describe it concretely."

The core request. A probability without a concrete downside description is missing exactly the part that determines whether the bet is worth taking.

> "Is the upside and downside of this decision actually symmetric, or does one side cost more than the other side pays? Be specific about the size of each."

Names the asymmetry directly, rather than letting a single success probability imply the two sides are comparable when they aren't.

> "What's our actual position — cash, options, morale — in the worst realistic scenario, not the average one? Would we still have real choices left, or would we be out of them?"

Tests whether the downside is survivable-but-painful or genuinely threatens the company's ability to keep deciding anything at all — which is the distinction that actually matters most.
