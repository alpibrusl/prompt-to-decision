# Reversible and Irreversible

Not every wrong decision costs the same to undo, and treating them as though they do is one of the most common ways good judgement gets applied at the wrong intensity.

## Two very different kinds of door

**Reversibility** is how much of a decision's cost you get back if it turns out to be wrong and you change course. Some decisions are close to fully reversible: hire someone, and if it doesn't work out within a few months, you part ways — a real cost, in money and in the disruption to the person's life, but a cost that stops accruing once you act. Other decisions are close to irreversible: sign a five-year office lease in Germany, and walking away doesn't make the commitment disappear — you're paying for it whether or not you're using it.

The useful image is a door. Some decisions are a door you can walk back through — reversible, worth trying quickly and correcting if you're wrong, because the cost of trying and being wrong is genuinely low. Others are a door that locks behind you — irreversible, or close enough to it that "we'll adjust if it doesn't work" isn't actually available to you once you've gone through.

## The mistake is applying one standard to both

Ledgerly's recommendation bundles several decisions that don't actually share a reversibility profile, even though they arrive as one package. Hiring is closer to the reversible door — costly to get wrong, genuinely disruptive to the people involved, but not permanent. A long lease, a large upfront marketing commitment in the new market, a public announcement that would be embarrassing to walk back — these are closer to the irreversible one, and they deserve a different standard of certainty before committing to them.

The mistake worth naming directly: treating a bundled decision as one door, at one uniform level of caution, when it's actually several doors of very different weights. A founder who is appropriately decisive about the hiring — move fast, correct if wrong, the cost of delay exceeds the cost of a mistake — can end up applying that same decisiveness to the lease, where the cost of being wrong is completely different and deserves more deliberation, not the same speed.

## Splitting the bundle

The fix is to pull a bundled recommendation apart into its actual components and ask about reversibility separately for each one, rather than accepting or rejecting the whole package at a single speed. Which parts of "hire six and expand into Germany" can be entered gradually, tested, and walked back if wrong? Which parts commit Ledgerly to something that doesn't unwind cleanly, regardless of what's learned along the way?

Frequently, the answer points toward a real, better option that wasn't in the original comparison at all: take the reversible steps first — hire the first two people, run a lean test in Germany without the office lease — and defer the irreversible commitments until the reversible ones have actually produced evidence. This is not caution for its own sake. It is matching the speed of a decision to how expensive it actually is to be wrong about it, which a single bundled recommendation, presented as one yes-or-no choice, obscures by default.

## What to ask for

> "Break this recommendation into its individual commitments, and tell me which ones are reversible if we're wrong, and which ones aren't."

The core request. A recommendation presented as one decision usually contains several, and they rarely share a reversibility profile.

> "For the irreversible parts specifically, what would make us confident enough to commit, given that we don't get to correct course afterward?"

Applies a higher bar specifically where the bar should actually be higher, rather than uniformly across a bundled plan.

> "Can we sequence this — take the reversible steps first, and defer the irreversible ones until we have real evidence from the reversible ones?"

Turns reversibility from a description into a plan: often the actual best move is not "do it" or "don't," but "do the part we can undo first."
