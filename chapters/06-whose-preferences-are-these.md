# Whose Preferences Are These?

Chapter 5 showed that a trade-off gets resolved to one point on a curve, whether or not anyone chose it deliberately. This chapter asks the question underneath that one: resolved according to whose values?

## The weighting is a values statement wearing a number's clothes

An **objective function** is the actual, precise thing being maximized once every competing goal has been given a weight — not "growth and runway both matter," but "growth matters roughly twice as much as runway, expressed as a single number that trades cleanly against the other." Somewhere inside every recommendation that resolves a trade-off, an objective function like this exists, whether or not anyone wrote it down or agreed to it.

That weighting is not a neutral computation. It is a values statement, and different people with a real stake in Ledgerly's decision would write a genuinely different one. A founder two years from burning out on the current grind might weight runway safety heavily, wanting room to breathe rather than another sprint. A founder who sees a narrow window before a competitor moves might weight speed heavily, willing to take real risk to not miss it. An investor with Ledgerly as one company in a diversified portfolio might reasonably weight upside over downside protection in a way that would be genuinely reckless for a founder whose entire net worth and years of work are concentrated in this one company. None of these weightings is objectively correct. They are different people's honest preferences, and a single recommendation can only ever encode one of them.

## Whose weighting did the recommendation actually use?

When an agent resolves a trade-off into one recommendation, it made a weighting choice, the same way it makes an objective choice when nobody stated one — usually by picking whatever weighting seems most implied by how the request was framed, or by defaulting to something that sounds balanced without actually being anyone's stated preference. "Balanced" is itself a choice, and it is worth asking directly whether it's the balance any actual person involved would have chosen, or a plausible-sounding default that happens to belong to nobody.

This matters most exactly where Chapter 5's trade-off was sharpest. Ledgerly's founders and Ledgerly's investor do not automatically share a risk appetite, even though they share a company. The investor's portfolio absorbs one failed bet more easily than a founder's entire career does. A recommendation that leans toward growth and speed may be genuinely optimal by the investor's weighting and genuinely too aggressive by a founder's — and unless someone asks whose preferences actually produced the number, the recommendation arrives looking like a single, neutral, correct answer instead of what it is: one specific person's risk tolerance, computed precisely and presented as if it belonged to everyone.

## Naming whose preferences you're using is not a formality

The fix is not complicated, and skipping it is exactly what makes this mistake so common: state, out loud, whose risk tolerance and whose priorities the recommendation should actually be built on, before asking for one. If it's the founders', say so, and say which founder's, if they genuinely differ. If it's meant to satisfy the board or the investor as well, say that too, and expect the recommendation to look different depending on the answer.

This is not about finding the objectively correct weighting — Chapter 2 already established that most of what this book covers doesn't resolve to one provably right answer. It's about knowing whose answer you're actually looking at, so that when it doesn't sit right, you can ask whether that's because it's wrong, or because it was never built for your risk tolerance in the first place.

## What to ask for

> "Whose priorities did you weight this recommendation against — mine, the company's abstractly, the investor's, some average? State it explicitly."

The core question. An answer that can't name whose weighting was used is a recommendation nobody has actually claimed ownership of.

> "Rebuild this recommendation weighted toward runway safety over growth speed, the way a founder near the end of their own risk tolerance might weight it. Show me how it changes."

Makes a specific, different set of preferences concrete, rather than leaving "whose weighting" as an abstract question.

> "If the investor and the founders would genuinely weight this differently, say so directly rather than picking one silently and presenting it as balanced."

Surfaces disagreement about values before it gets mistaken for disagreement about facts — which is, historically, one of the most common and most avoidable ways decisions go wrong inside a company.
