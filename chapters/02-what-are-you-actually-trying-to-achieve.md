# What Are You Actually Trying to Achieve?

Ask the agent to recommend how Ledgerly should grow, and it will optimize brilliantly for growth. That sentence should sound circular, because it is — and the circularity is exactly where this chapter lives.

## An objective is not obvious just because it's common

An **objective** is the goal a recommendation is actually optimized against — the thing "best" secretly means in "here's the best option." Growth. Runway. Profitability. Founder control. Preparing for a specific fundraise. These are all real objectives a real company might hold, and they do not point toward the same recommendation. A plan that's excellent for maximizing growth can be a bad idea if what Ledgerly actually needs right now is six more months of runway before the next round, and a plan that's excellent for extending runway can be a bad idea if the actual goal is proving the model can scale before a competitor gets there first.

The agent optimizing for "growth" did not make an error. Growth is a completely reasonable default objective for a company that has just raised a seed round — it's the goal most similar requests are actually asking about, so it's a sensible thing to assume when nothing else was specified. The problem is that *nobody specified it*. The objective got filled in, quietly, by whatever was most plausible given the request — which is a different thing entirely from being what Ledgerly's founders actually meant.

## Objectives compete, even when nobody says so

Ledgerly's founders, if you asked them directly, might hold several objectives at once, and they might not agree with each other. Grow the business, obviously. But also: don't run out of cash before the next raise. Also: don't get so diluted expanding that the round after this one costs more control than it's worth. Also, quietly, underneath the others: prove to themselves and their existing investor that the current model actually works before betting on a new one.

An optimization needs a single objective, or a stated way of weighing several against each other, to produce one clean recommendation. A person is allowed to hold several unresolved objectives at once and never quite rank them, because that's what it is to run a business under uncertainty. When you hand a request to an agent without resolving that ranking yourself, the agent doesn't leave it unresolved the way you might have. It picks — usually the objective most directly implied by the words you used — and hands back a confident answer built on a choice you never actually made.

## Whose review recognizes the goal, and whose doesn't

Here is a habit worth adopting before treating any objective as settled: ask two different people connected to the decision, separately, what they'd say Ledgerly is actually trying to achieve right now. Not what the recommendation optimized for — what they, personally, think the point is.

If a founder says "prove we can grow efficiently before the next round" and an investor says "grow as fast as the market allows," the recommendation that's optimal for one of those is not obviously optimal for the other, even though both people would nod along to "growth" as a one-word summary. The disagreement was always there. Optimizing quietly picked a side.

## The objective has to survive being said out loud

The fix here is not complicated, and it is worth doing before any recommendation gets requested, not after one comes back and feels off. State the objective explicitly, in one sentence, and ask whether everyone with a stake in the decision would actually sign it.

"Grow revenue as fast as possible" is a real objective, and a defensible one — for a company that has decided speed matters more than safety margin right now. "Extend runway to eighteen months while growing what we can" is a different, equally real objective, and it points toward a different recommendation from the exact same set of alternatives. Neither one is the objectively correct choice for every company in Ledgerly's position. The correct move is stating which one you're actually optimizing for, on purpose, before asking the agent to optimize for it — because if you don't state it, something gets chosen for you, silently, and it may not be what you'd have chosen yourself.

## What to ask for

> "What objective did you optimize this recommendation against? State it in one sentence, precisely enough that a different objective would have produced a different answer."

The core check. If the stated objective is vague enough that almost anything would satisfy it, that's the finding — a vague objective is one nobody actually chose.

> "If the objective were 'extend runway' instead of 'maximize growth,' would this recommendation change? Show me what it would look like."

Forces the sensitivity of the recommendation to its objective into the open, the way a load-bearing assumption gets tested in the third book of this series.

> "Which objective would each person with a stake in this decision — founders, the board, whoever it affects — actually state, if you asked them separately rather than assuming they agree?"

Surfaces disagreement about the goal itself before it gets mistaken for disagreement about the plan.
