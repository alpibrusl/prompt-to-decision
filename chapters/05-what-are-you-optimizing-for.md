# What Are You Optimizing For?

Chapter 2 was about naming the objective. This chapter is about what happens once you're honest that there isn't just one.

## Real objectives compete

A **trade-off** is a point where advancing one objective costs you ground on another — not a false choice, a genuine one, where more of one thing really does mean less of another. Ledgerly's founders want growth. They also want to survive to the next fundraise. Hiring six people and expanding into Germany advances the first and directly costs the second: it burns the exact runway that "survive to the next raise" depends on. There is no version of the plan that gets you more growth speed without spending more of the safety margin. That is not a flaw in the plan. It is what a real trade-off is.

The danger is not having competing objectives — every real decision has them. The danger is a recommendation that resolves the trade-off silently, choosing how much growth to buy with how much runway, without ever stating that a choice was made or what the alternative weighting would have looked like.

## The number the agent didn't show you

Ask for a recommendation, and an agent will very often quietly pick a point on the trade-off curve and hand you only that point — "hire six, expand into Germany" — without showing you the curve itself. Maybe hiring three people instead of six gets you sixty percent of the growth for thirty percent of the runway cost. Maybe hiring six but delaying the Germany office by one quarter changes the trade-off substantially. These are all real points on the same underlying curve, and a recommendation that shows you exactly one of them — without the shape of the whole thing — is asking you to trust a single choice you never got to see the alternatives to.

This is not a criticism of picking one answer; a recommendation has to land somewhere. It's a caution about accepting the landing point without seeing enough of the curve to know whether a nearby point would have served you better. Three hires and a slower Germany timeline might be a substantially better trade for Ledgerly's actual risk tolerance than six hires and full speed, and you cannot know that from a recommendation that only ever showed you one point.

## Making the curve visible

The fix is a direct request, not a complicated one: ask for the trade-off explicitly, not just the recommended point on it. "Show me growth versus runway cost at three, six, and nine hires" turns one confident answer into a real curve you can actually look at and choose from, rather than a single number dressed as the obvious choice.

This is worth doing even when — especially when — the recommendation sounds decisive. A confident single answer and a well-mapped curve with one point highlighted can both sound equally certain in a chat window. Only one of them lets you see what you're actually giving up by choosing where the agent chose, versus somewhere else on the same trade-off.

## What to ask for

> "What is this recommendation trading off against what? Name the two objectives in tension, and show me how much of one this plan costs in terms of the other."

The core question. If there's no real trade-off — if the recommendation is just strictly better on every objective that matters — that's worth knowing too, though it's rarer than it sounds.

> "Show me this same recommendation at a smaller scale and a larger scale — three hires, six, nine — and how the trade-off between growth and runway changes at each point."

Turns one point into a curve, so the choice of where to land becomes visible rather than assumed.

> "If we cared twice as much about preserving runway as about growth speed, would this recommendation change? Show me what it would look like instead."

Tests how sensitive the recommendation actually is to the weighting between objectives — the trade-off equivalent of the sensitivity check the third book in this series applies to a single number.
