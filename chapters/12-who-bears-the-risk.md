# Who Bears the Risk?

If Ledgerly's expansion fails, several different parties absorb the consequences, and they don't absorb them equally. That gap is worth examining directly because it changes what "acceptable risk" actually means, and it changes it differently depending on whose seat you're asking from.

## The same failure, landing differently on different people

An **externality** is a cost of a decision that lands on someone other than the person making it. Ledgerly's investor holds this company as one position in a portfolio built to survive several failures; a Germany expansion that doesn't work out is a loss, absorbed and diversified away across other bets. The founders hold no such portfolio — Ledgerly failing or nearly failing is not one loss among many, it's years of concentrated work and, often, a meaningful share of their own financial security. The six people hired to move fast on Germany hold something different again: a job, a decision to join based on the company's stated trajectory, and if the pilot doesn't clear its threshold, a layoff that costs them regardless of how sound the original decision was.

None of these people are wrong to hold the risk differently. They're structurally in different positions, and a recommendation that treats "the company's risk" as one uniform number obscures the fact that the same failure costs each of them something genuinely different.

## Whoever decides should feel some of what's at stake

The useful question this raises isn't about fairness in the abstract. It's a practical check on the decision itself: is the person actually making the call the person who bears its downside, or has the risk quietly been separated from the decision-making?

If a recommendation is being pushed by whoever is most insulated from the downside — an investor advocating for aggressive growth from inside a diversified portfolio, while the founders who'd actually absorb a failure are more hesitant — that's worth surfacing directly, not because the aggressive position is automatically wrong, but because a decision made mostly by people who don't bear its cost deserves more scrutiny than one made by people who do. The reverse is also worth naming: founders sometimes underweight a good bet because they, personally, can't afford to be wrong even once, which is a completely reasonable position for them to hold and a reason the company might genuinely be better served bringing in someone who can absorb more risk on this specific call.

## Naming who bears what, before deciding

The habit worth building here is not complicated: before committing to a recommendation with real downside, name explicitly who bears what if it goes wrong. The investor's loss. The founders' loss, in money and in years. The new hires' loss, if the pilot fails and roles get cut. Naming this doesn't resolve the decision by itself — reasonable people who've named the same risks honestly can still land in different places. But a decision made after this question was actually asked is a materially different thing from one where the risk was left as an abstract number nobody traced back to the specific people it would land on.

## What to ask for

> "If this goes wrong, name specifically who bears the cost, and how much of it lands on each — the investor, the founders, the people we'd hire."

The core question. A risk described only in aggregate — "the company" — is a risk nobody has actually traced to the people it would land on.

> "Is the person most in favor of this plan also the person who'd bear the most if it fails? If not, who is, and have they actually weighed in?"

Surfaces whether risk and decision-making authority are aligned, or quietly separated in a way worth noticing before committing.

> "For the people we'd hire specifically — what happens to them if the pilot doesn't clear its threshold? Is that an acceptable cost we're prepared to own, or one we're hoping not to think about?"

Names the externality that's easiest to leave unexamined, because the people it falls on aren't in the room when the recommendation gets accepted.
