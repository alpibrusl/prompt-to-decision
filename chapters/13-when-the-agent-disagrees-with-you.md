# When the Agent Disagrees With You

You push back on the recommendation. You say Germany feels too fast, that three hires and a slower timeline seems more sensible than six and a sprint. The agent doesn't just agree and revise. It holds its position, and re-explains why six and fast is actually the better call.

That moment is worth taking seriously, in both directions it can go.

## Disagreement that holds is a different signal than disagreement that folds

If an agent maintains a recommendation under real pushback — not stubbornly repeating itself, but engaging with your specific objection and explaining why it doesn't change the conclusion — that's genuinely useful information. It means the recommendation wasn't a shallow best-guess that any pressure would dislodge; something about the evidence or the reasoning is load-bearing enough to survive being questioned. Treat that the way Chapter 7 of the third book in this series treats a model that reproduces a known answer: not proof the recommendation is right, but real evidence it's not merely fragile.

The opposite is just as informative, and worth watching for just as carefully. An agent that revises its recommendation the moment you express doubt, without anything about the underlying evidence having changed, tells you the opposite thing: that the original recommendation was never load-bearing in the first place, and neither, probably, is the revised one. If disagreement with no new information can move the answer, the answer was never resting on much to begin with.

## The test: what actually moved it

The useful move, in either direction, is asking directly what would have to be true for the agent to change its recommendation — and then checking whether what you actually said matches that. If you raised a real concern that maps onto something the model didn't account for — a cost it hadn't included, an alternative it hadn't scored — and the recommendation changes because of that, the system is working exactly as it should: your objection surfaced a real gap, and the recommendation improved. If the recommendation changes because you simply sounded uncertain, with no new information having entered the conversation at all, that's not the system working. That's the appearance of responsiveness standing in for the substance of it.

This cuts against a natural instinct, worth naming directly: it's tempting to treat an agent that agrees with you easily as the more pleasant, more trustworthy one. It's often the opposite. A recommendation that never survives a real challenge was never worth much more than a guess dressed as an answer.

## Disagreement between people is worth exactly this same treatment

The same test applies when the disagreement isn't with an agent but with a cofounder, an investor, or a board member who's pushing back on a plan you believe in. Before treating their objection as noise to get past, ask the same question you'd ask of the agent: does their objection point at something the plan genuinely hadn't accounted for — a cost, an alternative, a risk they will carry that you won't — or is it a feeling with no new information behind it? The first kind should move the decision. The second is subtler with a person than with an agent: an agent's wobble under doubt is noise, but a cofounder's "this feels too fast" may be Chapter 6's finding arriving in plain clothes — a real difference in risk tolerance, which is information about whose preferences the plan is running on even when it carries no new fact about the plan itself. That deserves to move the weighting, not the evidence. Telling a new fact from a new preference from plain noise is the entire skill this chapter is about.

## What to ask for

> "I disagree with this recommendation. Before you revise it, tell me: does my objection point at something the model didn't account for, or does it change your recommendation without any new information? Be honest either way."

The core question. It puts the burden on identifying whether the disagreement carries real information, rather than letting either stubbornness or easy agreement stand in for that answer.

> "What would actually have to be true for you to recommend something different? Compare that against what I just said — does it match?"

Turns a vague sense of pushback into a specific, checkable claim about what should move the recommendation and what shouldn't.

> "Has this recommendation survived a real challenge yet, or has every version of it so far just been the first one nobody's seriously pushed on?"

Worth asking of any recommendation before committing to it — a plan that's never been genuinely argued with is a plan whose confidence hasn't actually been tested yet.
