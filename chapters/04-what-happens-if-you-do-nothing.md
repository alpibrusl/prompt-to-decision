# What Happens If You Do Nothing?

Of all the alternatives Chapter 3 said go missing from a comparison, one goes missing so reliably it earns its own chapter: not doing anything at all.

## The option that never gets a champion

The **status quo** is the alternative of continuing what's already happening, unchanged — the option nobody has to design, propose, or defend, because it's simply what continues by default if no decision gets made. That's precisely what makes it vanish from comparisons so easily: every other alternative had someone build a case for it, score it, put it in the table. The status quo just sits there, un-championed, competing against options that all had an advocate and it didn't.

Ledgerly's comparison was framed as Germany versus France. Notice what's absent: staying purely domestic, continuing to serve the market Ledgerly already understands, reinvesting the same runway into deepening the product feature that already tested well. That is a real alternative — arguably the most defensible one, since it's the only option built entirely on things already proven to work — and it never got scored, because the request that produced the comparison was already about which country to expand into, not whether to expand at all.

## Doing nothing is not the same as doing nothing risky

The reason the status quo gets skipped so easily is that it feels safe by default, which is a genuine trap in its own right. Staying domestic is not automatically the low-risk option. A competitor could be planning the same German expansion Ledgerly is considering, and the twelve months Ledgerly spends "staying safe" could be the twelve months that market closes. A feature that's working today can stop working as the market shifts. The status quo has its own risk profile — it is simply a risk profile nobody bothered to compute, because computing it wasn't what the original request asked for.

Treat the status quo the way any other alternative deserves to be treated: scored on the same objective, with the same honesty about what could go wrong with it, not assumed safe by default because it's the one nobody had to argue for.

## Why "at least we didn't do anything" feels like protection, and often isn't

There's a specific comfort in choosing the status quo that's worth being suspicious of on its own terms: it feels like the choice that can't be blamed later, because nothing was actively done. If the German expansion fails, the decision to attempt it will be scrutinized. If staying domestic means a competitor takes the opening instead, that cost rarely gets attributed to a decision at all — it just looks like something that happened to the market, not a consequence of choosing not to act.

This asymmetry is real, and it quietly biases toward the status quo more than its actual risk profile deserves, precisely because inaction's failures are harder to trace back to a moment someone chose. Naming that bias doesn't tell you which alternative is actually better for Ledgerly. It does mean the status quo shouldn't win by default just because it's the option that feels like it can't be blamed — it has to win, if it wins, on the same honest comparison everything else has to survive.

## What to ask for

> "Score the option of doing nothing — continuing exactly as we are — on the same objective and the same evidence as every other alternative. Don't assume it's safe; compute its actual risk."

The default request. If nobody can say what doing nothing would actually cost, that's the finding — it means the comparison was never really complete.

> "What's the realistic downside of staying domestic for another year — not the worst case, the plausible one? What would a competitor need to do to make that costly?"

Puts a real number, or at least a real scenario, behind the status quo's own risk, rather than letting it stand in as the default safe choice.

> "If we're leaning toward the status quo, is it because the evidence favors it, or because it's the option that's hardest to be blamed for later?"

The honesty check for the bias this chapter named. Worth asking of yourself as much as of the agent.
